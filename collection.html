TM·01 · History & mobility

The Tanda settlement

The Tanda is treated here as a cultural and social space rather than simply a settlement label. Public and community-oriented descriptions of Lambani/Banjara life frequently connect the Tanda with collective organisation, kinship, memory, and the continuity of community life.

Devotion and pilgrimage

Devotional practices and pilgrimage connect local community life with wider sacred geographies. The collection records this dimension without treating one religious practice as universal to every Lambani/Banjara family or region.

TM·06 · Festivals & social practice

Teej

Teej is included as a festival through which seasonal cycles, youth, community gathering, ritual activity, and hopes for the future can be remembered and transmitted. The archive keeps the description contextual rather than presenting a single version as universal.

TM·07 · Festivals & social practice

Seetla

Seetla-related practices appear in documentation of Lambani/Banjara religious life and are connected with ideas of protection, health, and collective ritual. Details can differ across locations and should therefore be read alongside their specific sources.

TM·08 · Festivals & social practice

Holi and communal celebration

Holi is represented as more than a visual spectacle of colour: it can operate as a space of gathering, song, performance, social memory, and intergenerational participation. The entry foregrounds the social dimension of celebration.

TM·09 · Festivals & social practice

Wedding traditions

Marriage practices form another important area of cultural continuity. This entry records wedding traditions as social processes involving families, kinship, ritual, clothing, food, music, and community participation, while recognising that customs vary.

TM·10 · History & mobility

Tattoos as markers of memory

Historical descriptions of Lambani/Banjara women sometimes discuss tattooing as a form of identity marking. In this archive, the subject is approached as a question about memory, mobility, and belonging rather than as an exotic visual feature.

TM·11 · Craftsmanship

Mirror-work embroidery

Mirror-work is one of the most recognisable elements associated with Lambani textile traditions. Small reflective pieces are incorporated into embroidery alongside dense stitch work, creating surfaces in which craft technique and visual identity meet.

Catalogue type · material culture · public craft documentation
TM·12 · Craftsmanship

Kangura and embroidered borders

Kangura refers to the distinctive triangular or zigzag border vocabulary found in Lambani textile traditions. The archive treats the border as a technical and aesthetic feature rather than reducing it to decoration: pattern can also carry recognition, continuity, and skilled making.

Catalogue type · textile technique · public craft documentation
TM·13 · Craftsmanship

Textile stitching and embellishment

Lambani embroidery brings together stitching, appliqué, mirrors, beads, colour, and carefully repeated motifs. This entry considers the broader textile-making practice and the knowledge required to produce and maintain it across generations.

Catalogue type · material culture · public craft documentation

No photographs are used.

The collection deliberately contains no photographs of community members or cultural objects. This is an ethical design decision: community members were not comfortable with sharing photographs, so the archive does not substitute internet images or stock photography in their place.

Reading the catalogue

Each item has an accession number, category, description, and catalogue type. The categories organise the archive without suggesting that cultural life can be reduced to separate boxes.

Future entries

New community narratives do not automatically become TM·14, TM·15, and so on. They enter through the participatory submission and review process described on the Interaction page.
